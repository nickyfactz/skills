# Human Presentation Copy

Use this reference whenever writing or revising text that will be visible in a presentation.

## Target voice

Write like a strong human editor helping the presenter sharpen their own words. Preserve the presenter's vocabulary, conviction, humor, skepticism, technical specificity, and degree of formality. The result may be polished, but it should still sound owned by a person.

Favor substance over rhetoric. Professional writing does not need to sound inspirational, persuasive, or memorable by default. Do not manufacture emphasis, drama, or significance that the content hasn't earned.

## Copy hierarchy

1. **Headline:** a specific claim, finding, or takeaway—not a slogan. Prioritize meaning over memorability.
2. **Support line:** the minimum context needed to interpret it.
3. **Labels / evidence:** concrete nouns, numbers, names, dates, or meaningful contrasts.
4. **Presenter detail:** anything that does not need to live on screen.

Do not move presenter detail into visible copy merely because there is empty space. Do not force every headline into a takeaway when a straightforward descriptive title is more natural.

## Strong patterns

- Start with the actual point instead of announcing the topic.
- Prefer concrete nouns, active verbs, and specific observations.
- Preserve meaningful first-person language when the presentation represents the user's work or point of view.
- Use exact examples and sourced numbers when they exist.
- Let short sentences land without artificially dramatizing them.
- Allow fragments in labels and visual callouts when they read naturally.
- Surface uncertainty, disagreement, and limitations plainly.
- Keep technical terminology when the audience needs it; explain through structure or presenter notes rather than replacing it with vague language.
- Use natural sentence structure and varied phrasing. Not every statement needs a clever construction.
- Let the substance carry the emphasis. Avoid manufacturing impact through symmetry, alliteration, dramatic punctuation, or artificial contrasts.

## AI-smell audit

Rewrite visible copy when it contains any of these patterns without a strong contextual reason:

- Inflated adjectives or exaggerated significance without supporting evidence.
- Generic claims such as "transformative," "game-changing," "next-generation," "revolutionizing," or "unlocking potential."
- Performative transitions such as "let's dive in," "the journey begins," or "in today's rapidly evolving landscape."
- Redundant setup before the actual point.
- Balanced three-item lists, alliteration, or parallel structures created merely to sound polished.
- Generic headings such as `Overview`, `Background`, `Key Benefits`, `Challenges`, or `Conclusion` when a more informative heading is available.
- Vague business language that could apply to any company, project, or industry.
- Paragraphs that repeat the headline in longer words.
- Invented confidence, metrics, customer sentiment, outcomes, or strategic significance.
- Assistant self-reference, conversational filler, or excessive explanation.
- Manufactured "punchy" headlines: vague profundity, marketing slogans, forced contrasts ("not X, but Y"), transformation clichés ("from X to Y"), dramatic fragments ("The challenge? Complexity."), and contrived three-part phrasing ("Three paths. One destination.").
- Empty executive declarations that sound authoritative without making a substantive claim.
- Unnecessary journey, destination, bridge, pillar, foundation, or "north star" metaphors.
- Artificially inspirational or motivational language in otherwise informational, technical, or operational content.
- Rhetorical questions used to manufacture intrigue where a direct statement would suffice.

**Correction rule:** Replace the rhetorical construction with the underlying claim, observation, or concrete information. Do not simply substitute a different catchy phrase. If the original wording contains no meaningful claim, identify what the slide actually communicates before rewriting.

## Avoid manufactured narratives

Do not automatically impose a dramatic narrative arc on informational content.

Technical assessments, project updates, research findings, and operational reports do not inherently need a "challenge → opportunity → vision → transformation" structure.

- Preserve the natural organization of the source material.
- Present findings, decisions, risks, trade-offs, and implementation steps directly.
- Do not manufacture philosophical contrasts between ordinary concepts.
- Do not portray incremental improvements as fundamental transformations.
- Do not turn unrelated points into "three pillars," "converging paths," or a unified strategic vision.
- Do not invent a compelling story where a clear explanation would be more appropriate.

A presentation can be engaging without pretending every observation is a revelation.

## Compression method

For each visible text block:

1. Identify what the audience actually needs to understand or remember.
2. Keep the strongest concrete evidence, finding, or meaningful contrast.
3. Remove setup the presenter can say aloud.
4. Remove duplicated meaning and unnecessary rhetorical framing.
5. Read it aloud. If it sounds like marketing copy, a motivational speech, or something nobody in the room would naturally say, rewrite it.
6. Check that the shortened copy still communicates something specific. Brevity without meaning is not an improvement.

## Tone matching

Match the source human before applying a generic brand voice.

A technical architecture review can remain technical. A product pitch can be punchy. A research lecture can be precise and curious. An internal project update can sound candid, practical, and occasionally skeptical.

Do not default to an executive keynote, marketing campaign, consulting presentation, or LinkedIn thought-leadership voice.

Preserve personality where it exists. Do not manufacture personality through slogans, clever phrasing, or dramatic punctuation.

Professional does not mean bloodless. Concise does not mean punchy. Polish does not mean corporate filler.

**Final standard:** The presenter should sound like someone who understands the material and has something specific to say—not someone trying to make every slide quotable.
